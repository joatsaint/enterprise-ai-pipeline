# The FAQ Bank

*Real questions, cleaned up and reformatted from actual landlord posts,
each answered in the same two-layer style already working live in Ernie
Garcia's Texas landlord Facebook group: a plain-English summary first,
then the professional answer with exact statute citations.*

See `DISCLAIMER.md`. This applies to every answer below.

---

## Q1 — My tenant has been in and out of the hospital for months with liver failure and unpaid rent. Does hospitalization pause the eviction process?

**Plain-English:** No. The law doesn't pause just because someone is sick.
You still have to follow every step exactly — but document generously,
since a judge may be more cautious with a hospitalized, unsupported
tenant.

**The real answer:**
1. **Notice to Vacate service** (§ 24.005): mailing it by certified *and*
   first-class mail satisfies § 24.005(f) even if the tenant can't
   physically receive it right away. A courtesy text doesn't count as
   legal service unless the lease says otherwise.
2. **Court service**: the constable/process server still attempts service
   at the property under Rule 510.4; a Power of Attorney doesn't
   automatically make that person a party to the case, and a hospital stay
   doesn't guarantee a postponement.
3. **Abandoned property**: if the tenant can't remove belongings, you
   still can't dispose of them outside § 54.045's procedure, and you still
   need a constable to execute the Writ of Possession — no self-help.
4. **Practical step**: keep a meticulous log of every attempt to
   communicate with the tenant and any power-of-attorney contact — it's
   your best protection if a judge questions whether you gave reasonable
   notice.

## Q2 — My tenant is being evicted by the constable on a set date. Can they be arrested for trespassing if they come back afterward?

**Plain-English:** Yes. Once the constable officially finishes the
eviction, the former tenant has no more right to be there — coming back
is trespassing, like sneaking back into a game after you've been told
you're out.

**The real answer:** Under **Texas Penal Code § 30.05**, entering or
remaining on property without consent, after notice that entry is
forbidden, is criminal trespass. Once a constable executes a Writ of
Possession under **§ 24.0061**, the former tenant becomes a legal stranger
to the property. Best practice: change every exterior lock the moment the
writ is executed, and explicitly tell the former tenant (in writing, if
possible) that they're no longer permitted on the property before
involving police for any return visit.

## Q3 — I saw furniture still in the unit near the end of the eviction timeline and moved a few items out myself. Was that legal?

**Plain-English:** No — like grabbing someone's toys off the playground
before the referee blows the whistle. Even if it's almost over and even if
it's technically your house, you can't touch their stuff until the
official final step happens.

**The real answer:** Under **§ 92.0081**, a landlord can't handle a
tenant's property outside of an executed Writ of Possession (§ 24.0061) or
verified abandonment — even if the tenant is severely delinquent, already
renting a U-Haul, or down to just a few leftover items. Doing so exposes
you to a civil suit for **conversion**, plus the same statutory penalty as
an illegal lockout: actual damages, one month's rent + $1,000, and
attorney's fees. If police are called and side with you on-scene, that
does **not** eliminate later civil exposure — it just means no arrest
happened that day.

## Q4 — Do I have to send actual receipts along with an itemized security-deposit deduction list, or is the written list enough?

**Plain-English:** The law only requires the written list, not the actual
store receipts. But most landlords keep and share receipts anyway, so
nobody can argue the numbers were made up.

**The real answer:** **§ 92.104(c)** requires a written description and
itemized list of deductions — the literal statute text does not require
attaching physical receipts or invoices. However, if the tenant disputes
the deductions in Justice of the Peace court under **§ 92.109**, the
burden of proof shifts to you, and you'll need real receipts/invoices to
back up the numbers. Keeping and proactively sharing receipts is a
litigation-risk decision, not a statutory requirement.

## Q5 — My hospitalized tenant says she wants to leave voluntarily but has no money or support to physically move out. What do I do?

**Plain-English:** She's stuck in bed and can't carry her own boxes. You
still can't just take your house back by force — talk to her, get it in
writing that she's giving it up, and figure out a safe way to get the keys
back.

**The real answer:** Keep every exchange in writing. Get explicit written
confirmation that she is voluntarily surrendering possession, and clarify
exactly how the keys will be returned or access authorized. You still
cannot enter, pack her things, or change locks until she's fully
surrendered the keys, or until you've obtained judgment and had a
constable execute a Writ of Possession under **§ 24.0061** — entering
early violates **§ 92.0081**. Document the medical circumstances
thoroughly; that documentation is what shows good faith if you end up
filing anyway once deadlines pass.

## Q6 — A family member let her niece move in informally (no written agreement), and now the niece won't leave when asked. What can she do? She's a tenant herself, not the property owner.

**Plain-English:** Like sneaking a friend into your treehouse against the
rules — if they won't leave, you can't just handle it yourself, because
your parent (the actual landlord) doesn't even know they're there, and you
broke the no-guests rule by letting them move in without asking first.

**The real answer:** Under **§ 91.005**, a tenant can't sublet without the
landlord's consent. Because the original tenant let the niece in
informally, the niece is likely an unauthorized occupant or unapproved
subtenant — but the tenant herself has **no legal standing to file an
eviction**, since only the property owner (or their authorized agent) can
file a forcible detainer suit. Self-help removal (changing locks, removing
the door, tossing belongings) is illegal regardless of who's asking.
Approaching the actual landlord to resolve it is an option, but it
exposes the original tenant to her own lease-violation risk for the
unauthorized subletting in the first place.

## Q7 — We just bought a 12-unit complex and inherited a tenant who isn't even on the lease, is 2 months behind ($1,300), and reportedly has multiple people living in the unit. What are our options?

**Plain-English:** Like discovering someone living in a new clubhouse
without a ticket and their name isn't on the sign-up sheet — you can't
just lock the door yourself; you have to serve the proper notice and let
the judge and constable handle it if she won't leave.

**The real answer:**
- Because she's not a party to any written lease with the new owners, she
  occupies as an unauthorized occupant/holdover.
- You still must serve a written **Notice to Vacate** under § 24.005
  (minimum 3-day statutory notice period for nonpayment/holdover) before
  filing a forcible detainer suit — you cannot bypass this even for an
  occupant with no valid lease.
- As the new owner, you're under no obligation to offer her a new lease.
- **Critical warning:** if the old property manager changed locks with
  police present without following § 92.0081's exact statutory lockout
  safeguards (advance written notice, available key, compliant lease
  language) or without an executed Writ of Possession, that's an illegal
  lockout — exposing the landlord to the same one-month's-rent + $1,000
  penalty regardless of how sympathetic the situation looks.
