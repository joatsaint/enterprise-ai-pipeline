# The 485 Moreland Playbook

*A real Texas landlord's eviction case, start to finish, with every
decision point turned into a lesson and every lesson backed by the actual
statute. Names and the property address are Randy's own real case as of
July–August 2026 — not a hypothetical.*

See `DISCLAIMER.md`. This applies to every lesson below.

---

## The setup

July 19, 2026. Rent on a rental property in Vidor, Texas was due on the
1st. Two days before this playbook opens, the landlord told the tenant the
house was going up for sale — an agent was already under contract, a
lockbox and a sign were coming. The tenant, already late on rent, asked
for time to pay, citing a family emergency that "wiped out his savings."
This was the second time a tenant had asked this landlord to float them.

That single moment — a tenant treating rent like a favor between friends
instead of a contract — is the entire playbook in miniature. Everything
that follows is about converting a personal relationship back into a
business transaction, on paper, using the law as the paper trail.

---

## Lesson 1 — Stop "floating" tenants without paper

**What happened:** The landlord had a habit of quietly granting informal
extensions ("give me a few days") with no documentation. This was the
second tenant to exploit that pattern.

**The lesson:** If you agree to any late-payment accommodation, put it in
writing — exact past-due balance, exact installment dates and amounts, and
an explicit statement that the agreement does **not** waive your right to
evict if it's broken. A verbal "sure, take your time" has zero value in
court and resets nothing in your favor.

---

## Lesson 2 — Never explain *why* you're taking action

**What happened:** The tenant asked, point-blank, "are you selling just
because I missed a payment?" The instinct — especially for a self-described
people-pleaser — was to explain and justify. That instinct is exactly what
creates legal exposure.

**The lesson:** Never link an adverse action (sale, notice, eviction)
directly to a tenant's specific behavior in writing or conversation. Keep
it to a neutral, factual, non-negotiable business statement:

> "My decision to sell the property is based on my financial position and
> my other existing financial obligations."

**Why this matters legally:** Under **Texas Property Code § 92.331**,
retaliation against a tenant is illegal — but only for a narrow set of
*protected* tenant actions (requesting repairs, filing a code-enforcement
complaint, joining a tenant organization). Non-payment is not a protected
activity, so acting on it is not retaliation. But if you volunteer a
narrative tying your decision to the tenant personally, you hand the
tenant a rope to try to pull on in court anyway. Stick to the boring,
impersonal, financially-true statement and repeat it verbatim if pressed.

---

## Lesson 3 — The Notice to Vacate is the mandatory first step, and it has real formatting rules

**What happened:** Before any eviction suit could be filed, a written
Notice to Vacate had to be served.

**The lesson — and the statute:** **Texas Property Code § 24.005** requires
a written Notice to Vacate before an eviction lawsuit can be filed. Valid
delivery methods:
- Hand delivery to the tenant or any occupant 16 or older, **or**
- Affixing it to the inside of the main entry door, **or**
- Mail (regular, registered, or certified).

A text message is **not** a substitute for legal service unless the lease
itself explicitly names electronic notice as valid. Certified mail is
**not** a statutory requirement — it's a best practice, not a legal
minimum.

**One overlooked requirement:** if you want to include a rent demand
inside the Notice to Vacate (a combined "pay or vacate" notice), § 24.005(i)
requires that you already sent a **prior separate written reminder** that
rent was due and unpaid. If you skip that reminder, a demand for rent
folded into the Notice to Vacate can be invalid.

## Lesson 4 — Use a process server, and don't sign the date yourself

**What happened:** The landlord was 90 miles from the property and didn't
trust USPS after prior lost mail. A process server (initially delayed by
car trouble, then a backup was engaged) delivered the notice in person.

**The lesson:**
- A process server is not legally required for the *first* notice (Notice
  to Pay or Vacate) — a real estate agent, or anyone else you designate,
  can deliver it. A certified/state process server matters more for later
  court paperwork (the citation).
- You (the landlord) should sign the notice yourself — a digital signature
  image applied to a PDF and emailed to your agent/process server to print
  is legally sufficient in Texas. No DocuSign-grade audit trail is needed.
- **The blank-date trick:** leave the vacate deadline date blank when you
  sign, and instruct whoever delivers it to fill in the date on the actual
  day of delivery, calculated as exactly three full days out. This
  prevents an invalid notice caused by a delivery delay after you already
  signed a fixed date.
- Get a sworn **Affidavit of Service** from whoever delivers it — this is
  the "gold standard" of proof if the case ever reaches a Justice of the
  Peace court.

## Lesson 5 — Block payment apps before a partial payment resets your clock

**What happened:** With the eviction process underway, the landlord had
to actively block the tenant on Cash App to prevent an unsolicited partial
payment from landing.

**The lesson:** In many Texas courts, **accepting a partial payment after
issuing a pay-or-vacate notice can waive your right to evict for
non-payment that month** — judges often treat it as an informal extension.
Cash App and similar apps often **auto-accept** incoming transfers by
default; simply not withdrawing the money is not reliably enough to prove
you rejected it. If your goal is a clean, unambiguous eviction record:
block the tenant on every payment app *before* you serve notice, so a
partial payment can't land at all.

## Lesson 6 — Never use "self-help" to remove a tenant or their property

**What happened:** This did not occur in the 485 Moreland case directly,
but it's the single most repeated warning throughout the underlying
research, and it's the mistake that turns a clean eviction into a
landlord's own legal liability.

**The lesson:** You cannot legally, at any point before an officer
executes a Writ of Possession:
- Change the locks
- Cut off utilities
- Remove or discard the tenant's belongings
- Prevent the tenant from entering

...even if the tenant has clearly moved most of their things out, rented a
U-Haul, or left only a few items behind. This is governed by **Texas
Property Code § 92.0081** (unlawful lockout / exclusion). Violating it
exposes you to actual damages, **a civil penalty of one month's rent plus
$1,000**, and the tenant's attorney's fees.

**The one narrow legal path to remove locks/access yourself:** if the
lease authorizes it and you follow the exact statutory lockout procedure
for a tenant delinquent on rent — advance written notice and a key made
available — but this is a narrow, strict exception, not a general license.

## Lesson 7 — The eviction lawsuit timeline, if it gets that far

**What happened:** The 485 Moreland case ultimately did **not** reach a
completed court eviction — it resolved through abandonment (Lesson 9).
This lesson documents the court path for cases that don't resolve that
early.

**The lesson — timeline and mechanics:**
1. If the tenant doesn't pay or vacate within the notice period, file a
   forcible detainer (eviction) suit in the Justice of the Peace court for
   the property's county.
2. Under **Texas Rules of Civil Procedure Rule 510**, the court sets a
   hearing **10–21 days** after filing. The tenant must be served with the
   citation by a constable, sheriff, or authorized process server — you
   cannot deliver eviction paperwork yourself. Service must occur at least
   6 days before the hearing.
3. A trial can't be postponed more than 7 days in total.
4. If you win, there's a mandatory waiting period (typically 24 hours)
   before a **Writ of Possession** issues, and a **5-day appeal window**
   after judgment.
5. Under **§ 24.0061**, the officer executing the writ must do so within
   5 business days of issuance, and must post a 24-hour warning notice
   before physically removing anyone.
6. You can only sue for **past-due rent** in the eviction suit itself.
   Late fees, utilities, or damages generally require a **separate**
   small-claims filing.

## Lesson 8 — Once a tenant is out, they're a legal stranger — but you still can't touch their stuff without a rule

**The lesson:** After the writ is executed and possession is returned to
you, the former tenant returning to the property is **criminal trespass**
under **Texas Penal Code § 30.05** — police can be called and the person
arrested. Change the exterior locks the moment the writ is executed.

Any belongings left behind are governed by **Texas Property Code
§ 54.045** (abandoned property after eviction) — there are required notice
and redemption-rights steps before you can dispose of or sell it. You
still cannot just throw it out immediately, even post-eviction.

## Lesson 9 — How 485 Moreland actually ended: abandonment, not a finished court case

**What happened:** A pay-or-quit notice and a notice of non-renewal were
both served (via process server, confirmed by the tenant's own texts that
he'd found them on the door). The landlord — 90 miles away and unable to
personally check the property — deliberately did not answer the tenant's
question about which notice controlled, to avoid the appearance of giving
legal advice or creating a conflict. By early August, the tenant had cut
off utilities without notice and stopped responding. The landlord posted a
**Notice of Abandonment** on the door and waited **5 days** before treating
the property as abandoned — exceeding his own lease's abandonment clause
as a safety margin.

**The lesson — the correction that matters most for this whole guide:**
The "X-day abandonment window" many landlords assume is state law is
**not a Texas statute.** Texas has **no single statutory abandonment
timeline.** It is a **contract term** — in this case, the TAR (Texas
Association of Realtors) lease's own abandonment clause (Paragraph
16B(3), using the "Notice of Abandonment" form, TAR 2224). **Check your
own lease's abandonment clause; do not assume a fixed number of days is
the law**, because it isn't — it's whatever your specific lease says.

**Why abandonment is often the real-world outcome, not a full court
eviction:** a tenant who has already stopped paying, stopped responding,
and cut their own utilities has usually already left in every practical
sense. Formal abandonment procedure (notice posted + lease-defined wait
period + no response) can resolve the situation without ever reaching a
hearing date — faster and cheaper than seeing a forcible detainer suit
through to a Writ of Possession, provided you follow your lease's specific
abandonment clause exactly rather than a number you assume is universal.

## The portfolio context this played out inside

This wasn't an isolated incident. At the time of this case, the same
landlord was actively managing an exit strategy across five Texas rental
properties (a FEMA buyout in progress, two HUD-guaranteed rentals, one
long-term condo tenant, and 485 Moreland itself) — after two separate
personal loans to "friends" (one in 1993, one in the 2020s, both roughly
$10,000) went bad in nearly identical ways. The throughline across both
the lending losses and the tenant-management lessons above is the same:
**empathy is not a currency, and every accommodation you make without
paper is a liability, not a kindness.**
